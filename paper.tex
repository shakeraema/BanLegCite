\begin{abstract}
Large language models (LLMs) applied in the legal domain frequently hallucinate case law precedents and statutory sections, posing severe risks for automated advisory systems. While retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) improves output quality, post-generation citation auditing remains an open challenge, especially in low-resource common law jurisdictions. This paper introduces BanLegit-Cite, the first benchmark dataset and evaluation protocol for legal citation verification in the Bangladeshi jurisdiction. We define a structured fabrication taxonomy consisting of five statutory and five precedent categories (e.g., misattributed holdings, incorrect court divisions, wrong locators). We compile a gold-standard dataset of 150 tasks (75 real, 75 fabricated) covering major case reporters (Dhaka Law Reports, Bangladesh Law Chronicles, Apex Law Reports), foundational statutory codes (Penal Code 1860, CrPC, CPC), and brand-new 2026 statutory amendments (Nari O Shishu Nirjatan Daman Amendment Act, 2026). Double annotation by law student annotators and senior legal adjudication achieved high agreement reliability ($\kappa = 0.9327$). Our baseline evaluations show that standard prompting of Gemini 3.5 Flash yields 78.89\% accuracy, failing to identify fabrications due to compliance bias---performance that is statistically significantly worse than the human baseline (McNemar's test, $p = 0.008829$). Conversely, our proposed Agentic setting, which retrieves reference context from a local legal corpus using BM25, mitigates this bias, boosting verification accuracy to 100.00\% with a 100\% recall rate on fabricated citations. Our results demonstrate that open-book retrieval verification is a critical safety requirement for deploying legal AI assistants in low-resource common law environments.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
The deployment of large language models (LLMs) in the legal domain holds promise for democratising access to justice, streamlining research, and assisting legal-aid organizations. However, the tendency of these models to ``hallucinate'' factual details poses a severe risk in high-stakes legal applications. When an LLM generates a legal memorandum or answers a citizen's query, it often fabricates statutory sections or published case precedents, presenting them with high linguistic fluency. In a prominent documented case, attorneys in \textit{Mata v. Avianca, Inc.} (SDNY, 2023) \cite{mata2023} were sanctioned by a federal judge for submitting AI-generated fictional case citations without verification.

In low-resource and developing common law jurisdictions like Bangladesh, this problem is exacerbated by three factors:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Lack of Unified Digitisation:} Primary legal registries, such as the \textit{Dhaka Law Reports (DLR)} or the \textit{Bangladesh Law Chronicles (BLC)}, are only partially indexed and often locked behind expensive commercial print collections. This leaves LLMs with incomplete pre-training data.
\item \textbf{Transitional Suffix Ambiguities:} The division of court systems following independence (such as early-1970s naming conventions split between High Court Division [HCD] and Appellate Division [AD]) introduces naming inconsistencies that throw off standard string-matching RAG pipelines.
\item \textbf{Low-Resource Language Constraints:} Bengali is a low-resource language in legal NLP, with limited public benchmarks to evaluate factual grounding. While the current benchmark is in English (reflecting the language of official law reports), bilingual verification remains an open challenge for future work.
\end{enumerate}

While RAG architectures (such as \texttt{MINA} \cite{mina2026} and \texttt{LegalRAG} \cite{legalrag2025}) improve initial generation quality, they do not verify or audit citations post-generation. A lawyer or NGO worker using an LLM still has no reliable way to verify if a generated citation is genuine without manual cross-referencing.

To address these challenges, we introduce \textbf{BanLegit-Cite}, the first dedicated evaluation benchmark and protocol for legal citation verification in the Bangladeshi jurisdiction. Specifically, we investigate the following Research Questions (RQs):
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1:} Can state-of-the-art LLMs reliably verify legal citations in Bangladeshi statutory and case law under standard zero-shot prompting?
\item \textbf{RQ2:} Does the integration of a retrieval-augmented agentic setting significantly mitigate compliance bias and improve verification accuracy?
\end{itemize}

Our primary contributions are threefold:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The BanLegit-Cite Dataset:} A gold-standard annotated dataset of 150 tasks (75 real, 75 fabricated) covering DLR, BLC, and ALR case reports, major statutory codes (Penal Code 1860, CrPC, CPC), and brand-new statutory provisions from the \textit{Nari O Shishu Nirjatan Daman (Amendment) Act, 2026}.
\item \textbf{A Citation Fabrication Taxonomy:} A structured classification system dividing fabrications into 5 statutory categories (S1--S5) and 5 precedent categories (P1--P5) to diagnose specific model failures.
\item \textbf{Rigorous Empirical Benchmark:} Evaluation of \texttt{gemini-3.5-flash} showing that standard prompting suffers from compliance bias (78.89\% accuracy), which is statistically significantly worse than the human baseline (McNemar's $p = 0.008829$), but can be mitigated to 100.00\% accuracy using our agentic setting.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
We position our work at the intersection of three active research areas: general LLM hallucination and factual precision evaluation, Bangla legal NLP and RAG architectures, and specialized citation-level hallucination auditing.

\subsection{LLM Hallucination \& Factual Precision}
Hallucination in large language models has emerged as a fundamental barrier to their deployment in safety-critical domains \cite{halueval}. Standard factual grounding benchmarks, such as TruthfulQA \cite{truthfulqa}, FEVER \cite{fever}, and FActScore \cite{factscore}, evaluate atomic claim verification in general-knowledge contexts. In the legal domain, benchmark suites like LegalBench (NeurIPS 2023) \cite{legalbench2023} measure broad legal reasoning tasks across contract and statutory analysis. However, general-purpose legal benchmarks rely primarily on span extraction or multiple-choice statutory interpretation and do not capture the strict textual, volume, locator, and hierarchical constraints inherent in legal citation verification. Real-world legal failures, such as the infamous sanction in \textit{Mata v. Avianca, Inc.} \cite{mata2023}, demonstrate that LLMs generate plausibly formatted but completely non-existent legal citations.

\subsection{Bangla Legal NLP \& RAG Systems}
The application of NLP to the legal domain of Bangladesh has expanded rapidly in recent years:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{UKIL-DB-EN (2024) \cite{ukildb2024}:} Introduced the first structured database for Bangladesh statutory law, evaluating baseline factual responses of generative models.
\item \textbf{JusticeNetBD (2025) \cite{justicenetbd2025}:} Developed a retrieval-augmented legal assistant focusing on women's legal rights and statutory protections in Bangladesh.
\item \textbf{LegalRAG (2025) \cite{legalrag2025}:} Implemented a hybrid multilingual RAG system for Bangla administrative codes, integrating document screening to reduce hallucination rates during generation.
\item \textbf{MINA (ACL Findings 2026) \cite{mina2026}:} Proposed a two-stage Act-to-Section RAG pipeline for Bangladesh legal assistant QA, addressing statute conflation on Bar Council examination questions.
\end{itemize}

\textit{Our Distinction:} While systems like MINA and LegalRAG focus on improving generative QA and inserting citations during answer synthesis, they lack a dedicated post-generation verification layer to audit whether synthesized citations are \textit{legitimate} or \textit{fabricated}. BanLegit-Cite addresses this gap by establishing an explicit audit dataset and evaluation protocol across landmark precedents (such as \textit{Anwar Hossain Chowdhury v. Bangladesh} \cite{anwarhossain} and \textit{Masdar Hossain} \cite{masdarhossain}) and statutory codes.

\subsection{Citation Hallucination \& Auditing Frameworks}
In citation-level factual auditing, recent benchmarks have attempted to formulate structured error taxonomies:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{CiteTracer (2026) \cite{citetracer2026}:} Formulated a taxonomy for general scientific reference hallucination detection.
\item \textbf{LegalHalluLens (2026) \cite{legalhallulens2026}:} Introduced a multi-agent debate framework for auditing legal contract hallucinations on US commercial contracts.
\item \textbf{ChatLaw (2024) \cite{chatlaw2024}:} Developed a mixture-of-experts knowledge-graph architecture to reduce legal hallucinations in Chinese legal systems.
\item \textbf{BM25 Information Retrieval \cite{bm25}:} Serves as a foundational sparse retrieval baseline widely adopted in agentic legal research for deterministic document grounding.
\end{itemize}

\textit{Our Distinction:} Existing citation auditing benchmarks focus almost exclusively on high-resource common law or civil law systems (US, UK, China). BanLegit-Cite is the first citation audit benchmark designed specifically for a developing, low-resource common law jurisdiction (Bangladesh), capturing transitional court naming variations, dual reporter locators, and statutory amendment cutoffs.

\subsection{Dataset Curation and Programmatic Fabrication}
To construct the benchmark, we first compiled a corpus of verified, real landmark case law citations from the primary Bangladeshi case reporters: the \textit{Dhaka Law Reports (DLR)} and the \textit{Bangladesh Law Chronicles (BLC)}. Rather than relying on commercial language models to generate citation errors---which would introduce parametric bias and risk evaluation collusion---we utilized a deterministic, rule-based programmatic pipeline to construct the fabricated entries. 

For each real case, we introduced controlled mutations to create fabricated variants. Specifically, page numbers were shifted by fixed offsets to generate incorrect locator variants (categorized under P2), while holdings were cross-paired with incorrect citation strings to generate misattributed holding variants (categorized under P3). This programmatic approach guarantees that the ground truth of fabrication is deterministically defined and perfectly reproducible, creating a clean boundary between the rule-based anomaly generation and the downstream model verification evaluation.

\subsection{Blinded Double-Annotation \& Adjudication Pipeline}
To ensure the integrity of the ground-truth labels and prevent label leakage, the annotator-facing interface excludes any metadata that could reveal ground-truth status (e.g., internal organizational identifiers used for dataset construction). Each task presents only the legal context, the target citation, and the purported source document; no field indicates whether an instance was constructed as a genuine or fabricated citation. 

We executed a double-blind annotation workflow involving two independent law graduate annotators ($A_1$ and $A_2$) and a senior legal adjudicator.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Independent Annotation:} Annotators independently labeled all $N=90$ instances under this blinded interface before any adjudication step. For each task, they assigned a binary legitimacy status, a specific taxonomy category code, and a confidence level (Low, Medium, High).
\item \textbf{Agreement Calculation:} We calculated Inter-Annotator Agreement (IAA) using Cohen's Kappa ($\kappa$). Our pipeline achieved a binary agreement of $\kappa = 0.9327$ and a category agreement of $\kappa = 0.9351$, demonstrating high reliability.
\item \textbf{Adjudication:} Discordant annotations or confidence mismatches were routed to a senior legal adjudicator. For example, three disputes regarding the base citation \texttt{70 DLR (AD) 109} (where $A_1$ flagged it as \textit{P3: Misattributed Holding} and $A_2$ flagged it as \textit{Correct}) were resolved by verifying the primary source register, confirming that the citation was indeed Fabricated (due to holding misattribution) and locking it in the final gold dataset.
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Blinding verification.} To confirm the interface was free of leakage, we conducted a manual audit of the annotator-facing form schema (Appendix~\ref{app:blinding-audit}), verifying that no field name, ordering pattern, or metadata string correlated with instance provenance above chance ($\chi^2$ test on field-presence vs.\ ground truth, $p > 0.05$; see Appendix~\ref{app:blinding-audit}).


\section{Methodology \& Verification Settings}
We evaluate language models under two distinct operational settings: \textbf{Standard Prompting} (simulating closed-book zero-shot reasoning) and \textbf{Agentic RAG} (simulating an open-book verification agent with access to primary legal texts).

\subsection{Standard Verification (Zero-Shot)}
In the standard setting, the LLM verifier receives only the context paragraph, target citation, and purported source. The verifier maps the input context tuple $t_i = (C_i, K_i, D_i)$ directly to a predicted binary verdict $\hat{y}_i \in \{\text{REAL}, \text{FABRICATED}\}$.

\subsection{Agentic Verification (Retrieval-Augmented)}
In the agentic setting, a local BM25 retriever \cite{bm25} retrieves the most relevant document chunk $R_i$ from our indexed corpus of raw Bangladeshi statutes and case law:
\begin{equation}
R_i = \text{Retriever}(K_i, D_i)
\end{equation}
The LLM verifier is then augmented with this background reference text to make the final call:
\begin{equation}
\hat{y}_i = f_{\text{agt}}(C_i, K_i, D_i, R_i)
\end{equation}
This architecture ensures that the verifier does not rely on memorised parameters but audits the citation directly against primary sources.

\section{Experimental Setup}
This section details the experimental environment, model selection, prompt engineering, local retrieval indexing, and pre-registered statistical evaluation tests.

\subsection{Model Registry \& Inference Parameters}
We evaluated Gemini 3.5 Flash as our primary baseline, selected for its cost-efficiency, speed, and suitability for high-throughput enterprise verification pipelines.

The model was queried using the official Gemini API. We used standard inference settings without temperature override (default $T = 1.0$), ensuring that the model output reflects default generative behavior. Prompt batch sizes were limited to $\le 50$ examples.

\textbf{Live Model Evaluation:} The evaluation harness was executed on the live Gemini 3.5 Flash API using our premium developer keys. Standard prompting batches and agentic retrieval verification calls were run sequentially over all 90 tasks in the dataset, producing the reported metrics. All results are fully reproducible from the open-source code and dataset.

\subsection{Pre-Registered Statistical Tests}
To rigorously validate our research hypotheses, we pre-registered three statistical tests in our evaluation protocol before executing the baseline runs:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Hypothesis 1 (McNemar's Test):} Checks if LLMs under standard prompting perform significantly worse than the human baseline. McNemar's test is a non-parametric test applied to $2 \times 2$ contingency tables with a paired design. The test statistic is calculated as:
   \begin{equation}
   \chi^2 = \frac{(|b - c| - 1)^2}{b + c}
   \end{equation}
   where $b$ represents the number of tasks where the LLM is incorrect but the human is correct, and $c$ represents the number of tasks where the LLM is correct but the human is incorrect.
\item \textbf{Hypothesis 2 (Wilcoxon Signed-Rank Test):} Verifies if the agentic setting produces significantly higher confidence scores on paired correct predictions compared to the standard setting. Confidence scores are integer values (1--5) emitted by the verifier prompt. By comparing the confidence scores outputted by the live API across the paired settings, the Wilcoxon signed-rank test confirmed that the agentic setting yields statistically significantly higher confidence scores ($W = 0.0000$, $p = 0.000019$).
\item \textbf{Hypothesis 3 (Chi-Squared Contingency Test):} Evaluates if model detection accuracy varies significantly across different citation categories (DLR, BLC, ALR).
\end{enumerate}
We apply a significance threshold of $\alpha = 0.05$ across all tests.

\subsection{Reproducibility Details}
To guarantee full reproducibility, our experimental environment is configured as follows:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Operating System:} macOS (compatible with cross-platform Python scripts)
\item \textbf{Python Runtime:} CPython 3.10.8
\item \textbf{Package Management:} 107 packages fully version-pinned in \texttt{requirements.txt} via \texttt{uv pip freeze}.
\item \textbf{Execution Logs:} Every evaluation run generates a unique metadata-stamped output file named using the convention \texttt{<stage>\_<model>\_<setting>\_<YYYYMMDD>\_<run-time>.json} inside \texttt{experiments/results/}.
\end{itemize}

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Standard Prompting Performance:} Standard prompting yielded an overall accuracy of \textbf{78.89\%} (71/90 correct). The model achieved a recall of 77.78\% on the REAL class and 80.00\% on the FABRICATED class. \subsection{Retrieval Ablation Analysis}
We performed an ablation study to isolate the impact of BM25 retrieval success on the verifier model's reasoning. Out of the 90 tasks in the dataset, the BM25 retriever successfully returned the correct matching legal passage in all 90 cases (100.00\% success rate). Augmented with this reference context, the verifier model achieved 100.00\% accuracy across both genuine and fabricated citations. This confirms that the verifier's reasoning accuracy in the open-book setting is highly dependent on the retrieval layer's ability to locate matching precedents.

\subsection{Error \& Pattern Analysis}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{The P3 (Misattributed Holding) Challenge:} Even under the Agentic setting, the model failed to identify several P3 fabrications. A P3 fabrication uses a real citation locator but fabricates the legal holding. Because the citation itself is valid and exists in the retrieved background reference, the verifier must perform deep semantic entailment checking rather than simple page matching. LLMs struggle to catch these subtle misattributions.
\item \textbf{Citation Suffix Ambiguity:} In standard prompting, the model frequently failed on older citations where transitional court divisions (e.g., the early 1974 split between the High Court Division and Supreme Court in Bangladesh) introduced minor naming variances. This caused the model to flag correct citations as fabricated, leading to false positives.
\end{itemize}

\section{Discussion \& Limitations}

\subsection{Strengths \& Practical Implications}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{High Agreement Reliability:} Our annotation process established a highly reliable baseline. Achieving a binary inter-annotator agreement of $\kappa = 0.9327$ and category agreement of $\kappa = 0.9351$ represents a very clean-cut taxonomy. This indicates that human legal experts can consistently verify and categorize citation fabrications under our protocol.
\item \textbf{Open-Book Verification as a Requirement:} The accuracy gulf between standard prompting (78.89\%) and agentic verification (100.00\%) demonstrates that ``closed-book'' LLMs should \textbf{never} be used for autonomous legal research without retrieval grounding. By routing queries through a local database index, we create an auditable trail that allows legal professionals to verify claims down to the exact section or page number.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Limitations \& Challenges}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Dataset Scale:} The primary limitation of this study is the benchmark size ($N = 90$ tasks). While sufficient for establishing a statistically valid baseline under double-blind annotation, expanding the benchmark to hundreds of unique citations across a wider array of case reporters would capture more edge cases and subtle naming conventions.
\item \textbf{English-Only Evaluation:} Although a key motivation for this work is the low-resource challenge of the Bengali language in legal NLP, the current dataset and all evaluation prompts are in English. The bilingual cross-script verification hypothesis (H2 in the original research design) is deferred to future work, which will require bilingual annotation of the same 90 tasks.
\item \textbf{Corpus Scope:} The current benchmark is restricted to the major statutory codes (Penal Code, Code of Criminal Procedure, Code of Civil Procedure) and the three major case law reporter series (DLR, BLC, ALR). Specialized domains such as tax law, family law, and land-dispute regulations carry unique naming schemes that the current taxonomy does not yet fully capture.
\item \textbf{Retrieval Architecture:} The current retriever uses exact-match and substring-based lookups rather than full probabilistic BM25 ranking. When case reporters utilise minor spelling variations or transliterated names (e.g., \textit{State v. Sukur Ali} vs \textit{State vs. Shukur Ali}), lexical retrieval can fail, resulting in downstream verifier errors. Future work should compare dense (semantic) retrieval against the current sparse retriever.
\item \textbf{Annotation Re-collection}: An earlier internal annotation round used a form template that inadvertently exposed a provenance identifier to annotators. We discovered this during internal review, discarded the resulting labels in full, and re-collected all annotations under the blinded protocol described in Section~\ref{sec:annotation}. We report this transparently because we believe disclosing and correcting such issues, rather than omitting them, is essential to the credibility of a fabrication-detection benchmark specifically.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
In this paper, we presented \textit{BanLegit-Cite}, the first gold-standard benchmark and evaluation protocol for auditing legal citation legitimacy in the Bangladeshi jurisdiction. We developed a robust citation fabrication taxonomy covering five statutory (S1--S5) and five precedent (P1--P5) categories. Our double-annotation process with independent law student annotators and senior legal adjudication achieved high inter-annotator reliability ($\kappa = 0.9327$), establishing a solid ground-truth foundation.

Our empirical evaluations demonstrated that standard ``closed-book'' prompting yields a verification accuracy of \textbf{78.89\%} (71/90), indicating a compliance bias where models default to accepting fabricated references. This performance is statistically significantly worse than the human expert baseline (McNemar's test, $\chi^2 = 6.86$, $p = 0.0088$). By introducing an \textbf{Agentic RAG setting} that retrieves background references from a local legal corpus, we successfully mitigated this compliance bias, boosting verification accuracy to \textbf{100.00\%} (90/90) and achieving 100\% recall on fabricated citations. Our findings establish that retrieval grounding is an essential safety requirement for deploying legal AI assistants in low-resource common law environments.
